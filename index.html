Women are being locked in, locked out and pushed out

Forced to resign

A woman who falls pregnant shortly after starting a new job could be forced to resign, or work throughout pregnancy and childbirth.

Locked out of the job market

The 12-month test means that pregnant women are effectively unemployable, because they won’t be able to take leave by their due date.

Stuck in jobs they’ve outgrown

Women who are trying to get pregnant can’t change jobs for fear of falling in that 3 month gap – this isn’t just bad for gender equality, it’s also bad for productivity.

More likely to experience pregnancy discrimination

Those with less than 12-month service were 5 times more likely to report pregnancy discrimination – that they were dismissed because they’re pregnant.

Miss the cut, and you also miss out on key protections

No paid leave if her job is unsafe

She can be told to stop work if her job is unsafe while she’s pregnant, but she won’t get paid leave. This particularly affects women in industries like health care, manufacturing and construction.

No leave after a miscarriage

She isn’t entitled to special parental leave for pregnancy loss. Over 100,000 Australian women experience pregnancy loss each year.

No leave when she’s sick

She can’t take special parental leave for a pregnancy-related illness, like debilitating morning sickness. Women have been put on a performance plan for missing work, even with a medical certificate.

No right to request flexible work

She can’t request things like changed hours or working from home, while she is pregnant or after she has her baby. This affects parents of all ages, and can keep them trapped in jobs because they need flexibility.

Who’s hit the hardest?

Casualised and lower-paying industries

53% of casual employees work in retail, hospitality or health care and social assistance. High turnover makes 12 months with one employer hard to reach, and casuals must also show regular and systematic work.

Fixed-term sectors

10% of employees in education and training are on fixed-term contracts, and 6% in public administration and safety and in arts and recreation. The average is 4%. At universities, 36% of academic staff were fixed-term in 2025.

Women with fertility issues

1 in 6 Australian couples take more than 12 months to conceive. Changing jobs restarts the clock, and without 12 months’ service they can’t request flexible hours for fertility treatment.

Young mothers

32% of mothers under 25 had under 12 months’ service before the birth, compared with 12% of mothers over 25.

What about businesses and the economy?

In 2026, the UK government expanded day one parental leave to dads and partners. Its economic assessment found the costs to businesses were comparatively small – and that the change could even improve productivity.

How to ease it in

  1. Phase it inDrop the eligibility period from 12 months gradually over time – 40 weeks will close the gap.
  2. Pregnant women firstShorter waiting periods could be extended to pregnant women first.
  3. Then dads and partnersExtend the same shorter wait to men and other partners.
  4. Support small businessSmall businesses could be offered transition periods.
A Kiwi example

New Zealand has gradually improved access to parental leave. Since 2016, employees with 6 months’ service can take 6 months’ leave, and those with 12 months’ service can take 12 months’. New starters can request leave too.

What the experts say

In 2026, researchers and legal services told a parliamentary inquiry into the National Employment Standards what the 12 month test does. Here is what each said.

Professor Sara Charlesworth and Dr Iain Campbell

RMIT University

Say several National Employment Standards, including unpaid parental leave, are only accessible after 12 months’ service, so employees with shorter tenure are unfairly excluded.

Submission 20

Professor Marian Baird and Dr Alexandra Heron

University of Sydney Business School, work and organisational studies

Say unpaid parental leave should be a right from the start of employment, or at the very least should match the shorter eligibility period for paid parental leave. They describe the mismatch between the two as clearly inconsistent legislation that causes inequity and injustice in individual cases, and point to the UK, where unpaid parental leave is a right from the start of employment.

Submission 27

Working Women’s Centre NSW

Women’s Legal Service NSW

Reports helping many clients who were made redundant just before, or soon after, returning from parental leave. Recommends that the employer carry the onus of showing a dismissal during pregnancy, parental leave or the six months after return wasn’t connected to them. Also says the 12 month minimum for flexible work requests works against women with caring responsibilities.

Submission 83

Dr Emma Graham

Postdoctoral Research Fellow, QUT Centre for Decent Work and Industry

Recommends removing or significantly shortening the 12 month service rule for unpaid parental leave and for special parental leave. Her research found that short service is a significant driver of discriminatory job loss for mothers. She also recommends banning the dismissal or redundancy of pregnant employees and those on or just back from parental leave, unless the employer shows exceptional circumstances.

Submission 63

Dr Amanda Selvarajah

Monash University, labour law

Argues the 12 month rule is out of step with the Fair Work Act’s gender equality objective. She says it creates a gap where a worker can qualify for parental leave pay, from the government or an employer, yet have no right to return to her job. Of the reform options she sets out, she says removing the 12 month threshold would be the most positive.

Submission 17

Australian Nursing and Midwifery Federation

Australia’s largest union

Told the committee’s 10 June 2026 hearing (this was oral evidence, not its written submission) that the 12 month requirement should be removed so unpaid parental leave is available from the start of employment. Its example: a new employee could fall pregnant two months into a job, give birth at the 11 month mark, and fall one month short. It also says unpaid no safe job leave for employees under 12 months should be abolished or, if the rule stays, paid.

She did international comparisons drawing on the International Network on Leave Policies & Research 2026 report, which compares parental leave laws across 53 countries. Not all US states and Canadian provinces are captured, so the rest come from a search of public laws.

This campaign also draws on recommendations from experts and agencies, including those made to the ongoing Parliamentary Inquiry into the National Employment Standards:

Professor Sara Charlesworth and Dr Iain Campbell, RMIT University

Two RMIT University experts in employment law and industrial relations told the inquiry that several basic work entitlements, including unpaid parental leave and the right to request flexible work, only apply after 12 months with an employer (or, for casuals, 12 months of regular work). People with shorter service are unfairly left out. Using Australian Bureau of Statistics figures, they report that in February 2025, 17% of Australian employees had worked for their employer for less than 12 months: 18% of women and 16% of men.

Dr Alexandra Heron and Professor Marian Baird, University of Sydney

Two University of Sydney industrial relations researchers told the inquiry that unpaid parental leave should be a right from the start of a job, or at the very least should match the shorter eligibility period for paid parental leave. Unpaid leave currently needs 12 months with an employer, while government paid parental leave only needs 10 of the 13 months before the birth and at least 330 hours of work, about one day a week. They called this clearly inconsistent legislation that can cause inequity between employees and injustice in individual cases.

Dr Emma Graham, QUT

A postdoctoral research fellow in work and regulation at Queensland University of Technology told the inquiry that the 12-month rule should be removed or significantly shortened. Analysing the Australian Human Rights Commission’s national survey of parents, she found that mothers with less than 12 months’ service were almost five times more likely to report being dismissed because of pregnancy, parental leave or family responsibilities than mothers with more service.

Dr Amanda Selvarajah, Monash University

A labour law academic at Monash University told the inquiry that the 12-month rule should be removed. She said most employers who offer their own paid leave copy the 12-month rule. Others offer more generous policies, but these people can be exposed under current laws because they have no guaranteed right to their job back.

Working Women’s Centre Australia

The national peak body for Working Women’s Centres, which give women free legal advice and representation on workplace problems, told the inquiry that the 12-month eligibility rule for unpaid parental leave should be cut to 6 months. It said the rule discourages job mobility and reduces opportunities for wage growth, at a time when women’s earnings fall significantly behind men’s in the first five years after childbirth. It noted that comparable countries, including the UK, are moving to remove qualifying periods, and recommended giving small businesses extra time to introduce the change.

Australian Nursing and Midwifery Federation

Australia’s largest nursing and midwifery union told the Committee’s hearing on 10 June 2026 (oral evidence, not its written submission) that the 12 month requirement should be removed, so unpaid parental leave is available from the start of employment. Separately from the inquiry, ANMF Victoria’s parental leave guide for members shows it already works in practice: Victorian public sector nurses and midwives who aren’t casuals need zero months’ service to get parental leave, instead of the usual 12.
